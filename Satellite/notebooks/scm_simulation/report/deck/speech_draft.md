# Speech draft for the SCM Skill Formats deck

One section per slide in the deck's current order. Slide 4 is hidden in the deck.

**1. Cover**

The question is which way of packaging synthetic control as an agent skill gives a language model the best estimate of a treatment effect. A skill is a folder of instructions, reference documents and scripts. A language model reads it and runs it through a bash shell, one command per reply. Four skill formats were built for the synthetic control method of Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller, run through the Synth R package that two of the paper's authors wrote. Three language models used each format to estimate the effect of a treatment on one treated unit in two simulated panels. The panels are simulated, so the treated unit's outcome without treatment is known in every period, and so is the effect. Every answer is scored on the evaluation rows of the collaborator's simulation README, with the formulas of the collaborator's evaluation code.

**2. Method**

Start with the estimand. One unit is treated from period 41. In each treated period t, the effect on that unit is α₁ₜ = Y₁ₜᴵ − Y₁ₜᴺ, its outcome under treatment minus the outcome the same unit would have had without treatment. After period 40 the treated unit's observed outcome is Y₁ₜᴵ, so the missing quantity is the potential outcome Y₁ₜᴺ in periods 41 to 60. The deck calls the average of α₁ₜ over those 20 periods the ATT. With one treated unit, it is the average effect on that unit over the treated window.

Synthetic control estimates Y₁ₜᴺ by a weighted average of donor outcomes, Σⱼ wⱼYⱼₜ. The weights minimize (X₁ − X₀W)′V(X₁ − X₀W). X₁ holds the treated unit's predictors and X₀ the donors' predictors. Every weight is at least zero and the weights sum to one. So the synthetic control's predictors lie in the convex hull of the donors' predictors. V is diagonal with positive entries and sets how much each predictor counts. When V is searched, Synth chooses the V whose weights give the smallest mean squared prediction error of the outcome over chosen pre‑treatment periods. The weight problem sits inside the V search.

The method leaves three choices to the analyst. The first is which predictors enter X and over which periods each one is summarized. The second is how V is set. The third is which units form the donor pool. The skill formats differ in who makes these choices.

**3. Data**

Both processes come from the paper. DGP 1 is the paper's equation 1, a linear factor model. Y_itᴺ = δₜ + θₜ′Zᵢ + λₜ′μᵢ + εᵢₜ. Here δₜ is a common time effect. Zᵢ holds two observed covariates that stay fixed over time, with coefficients θₜ that change over time. μᵢ holds four unobserved factor loadings on common factors λₜ. εᵢₜ is a shock independent across units and periods, normal with standard deviation σ. Every unit shares δₜ, θₜ and λₜ, so the donors carry information about the treated unit's untreated path through these shared terms.

DGP 2 is the paper's equation 5, a dynamic model. Each unit's covariates move with its own lagged outcome, and its outcome follows Y_{i,t+1}ᴺ = 0.5 Y_itᴺ + β′Z_{i,t+1} + u_{i,t+1}. The coefficients are the same for every unit and every period, and every shock belongs to one unit. Each unit's series is drawn independently of every other unit's series, the treated unit's included.

Each panel holds 30 units, the treated unit and 29 donors, over 60 periods. The generator subtracts 3 from the treated unit's untreated outcome in each of periods 41 to 60 and leaves every donor untreated. So α₁ₜ = −3 in every treated period, the ATT is exactly −3, and treatment reaches no donor. In DGP 2 the recursion runs on untreated outcomes, so the treatment never enters the treated unit's later covariates or later untreated outcomes.

The noise level σ scales εᵢₜ in DGP 1 and uᵢₜ in DGP 2. The generator draws standard normal shocks once and then multiplies them by σ. So the three panels of a process share every factor and every shock and differ only in the scale of the outcome shocks. In DGP 2 the shocks u drive the whole autoregression, so a lower σ also shrinks the systematic path that the donors must match. The models see only the observed panel, with the outcome, the two covariates and the treatment group. The untreated path and the effect sit in a separate ground truth file that is used only to score finished answers.

The figure shows the treated unit's true untreated path and the README Section 3 synthetic control over all 60 periods at σ = 1.

**5. Scoring**

The scoring starts from one quantity. An answer's estimate is ATT̂ = (1/20) Σ_{t=41}^{60} (Y₁ₜ − Ŷ₁ₜᴺ). It is computed from the answer's counterfactual path, exactly as the collaborator's code computes it from the synthetic path. In those periods Y₁ₜ = Y₁ₜᴺ − 3, so ATT̂ + 3 equals the mean counterfactual error over the treated window.

Each row is read the same way. For one answer, the ATT bias row holds its estimation error, ATT̂ + 3. The README column averages that error over 1,000 replications, which estimates the bias of the Section 3 estimator. For one answer the ATT RMSE row is the absolute error, and the README column is the root mean square of the error over replications. The counterfactual RMSE compares the answer's path with the true untreated path over periods 41 to 60. It needs the ground truth, so it exists only in simulation.

Random noise is σ√(1 + Σⱼŵⱼ²), computed at the answer's own weights. Suppose the weights matched the shared part of DGP 1 exactly. The counterfactual error in a period would then be ε₁ₜ − Σⱼwⱼεⱼₜ, and with independent shocks its standard deviation is this formula. The 1 is the treated unit's own shock, and the sum of squared weights is what the donor average adds. Under DGP 2 the README uses the same formula as a reference level, since the shocks propagate through the autoregression.

Pre‑treatment RMSPE is the in‑sample fit over periods 1 to 40. Covariate balance is the root mean squared gap in Z1 and Z2 at period 40. The two within rows say yes or no for an answer, and the README column gives the share of replications within the bound. Donors carrying weight counts weights above 0.001.

The last two rows use only observable data. For CV‑RMSE, each pre‑treatment period s is left out in turn, the fit is redone without it, and the treated unit's outcome at s is predicted. The root mean square of those 40 out‑of‑sample errors is divided by the in‑sample RMSPE. A ratio above one measures how much worse the specification predicts held‑out pre‑treatment periods than the periods it was fitted on. For the last row, each donor with weight above 0.001 is dropped in turn, the fit is redone, and the largest change in ATT̂ is reported. The refits of an answer rerun its own specification and search V again wherever the answer searched V. The collaborator's code keeps V at the identity, which is its specification. The README column averages these two rows over 100 replications.

For a configure or design answer, the measures use the fitter run that produced the answer. For a profile answer, a Synth refit of the script's specification reproduces the answer within 0.0001 in every period.

**6. Skill formats**

The four formats differ in who writes the specification and who writes the code. Under scm‑configure, the model writes a specification in 13 Synth arguments, and a fitter runs the paper's code, Synth 1.1‑10, unchanged. Convex weights and no penalty are fixed. Under scm‑design, the model makes four choices that the paper names on page 496. These are the predictors and their periods, how V is chosen, convex or extrapolation weights, and a distance penalty with its size. The fitter turns the four choices into Synth's arguments, keeps all 29 donors and Synth's default optimizer and solver settings, and runs a native fit when the weights extrapolate or a penalty is used.

The two profile formats move the specification away from the solver. Before any solver run, a generator model wrote the skill, including an R script, from the paper and Synth's source. GPT‑5.4 wrote one and DeepSeek‑V4.1‑Flash wrote the other. The GPT‑5.4 script fixes the covariates averaged over the pre‑treatment periods and the outcome in every pre‑treatment period, and it searches V. The DeepSeek script fixes convex weights and offers options for predictors, outcome lags, V and optimizers. Under configure and design, the model makes the analyst's choices at run time. Under a profile skill, the generator made them when it wrote the skill, and every solver model inherits them.

Every model runs a skill the same way. The three solver models are gpt‑oss‑120b, OpenAI's open‑weight model, and GLM‑5.3, both served on Virginia Tech's ARC inference service, and GPT‑5.5 through OpenAI's API. All three run with the reasoning effort parameter set to low. At σ = 1 a reply could use up to 16,000 tokens under scm‑configure and the profile skills, and every one of those replies ended before that limit. The scm‑design runs and every run at σ = 0.5 and 0.2 set no reply limit, so the provider's default maximum applied, and every ARC call was streamed.

Each case is one conversation. The system message says, verbatim, that the model is operating a bash shell in a working directory that contains a skill, that each reply is executed as one bash command block and its output comes back in the next message, and that nothing outside the working directory is visible and there is no network. The first user message is the task on the slide. It names the panel file, the treated unit, the treated periods and the donors, and it asks for the untreated outcome in each of periods 41 to 60 and the average effect. Under scm‑configure and scm‑design it then names the skill with its description. The first message for a profile skill leaves that line out. After each reply the harness takes one bash block, runs it with a limit of 120 seconds, and returns the output. Any further blocks in that reply are ignored, and the model is told so. After every step the harness checks the answer file. A valid answer ends the case. An invalid one gets the problem appended to the output so the model can fix it. A case also ends after 12 steps.

The shell runs inside a bubblewrap sandbox with every namespace unshared, so there is no network. The model sees only its working directory, with the skill and the panel, plus R 4.3.3, Synth 1.1‑10 and Python. The ground truth never enters the sandbox, and the fitter's run log goes to a harness folder outside the working directory. Each model ran each case once. At σ = 1 that makes eight cases for gpt‑oss‑120b and GLM‑5.3, the two panels under the four formats, and six for GPT‑5.5, which has not run scm‑design.

**7. scm‑configure**

This is the skill's instruction file, SKILL.md, the first thing the model reads. The folder also holds a method reference, a settings reference with each field's type, range and rules, a data viewer and the fitter. The SKILL.md workflow has five steps. Step one looks at the data. The viewer prints the treated unit and the donors period by period, for the outcome or for either covariate. Step two reads the two references. Step three writes the 13 settings to a file and runs the fitter. Step four may revise the settings and run again. Step five writes the answer, with a counterfactual value for each of periods 41 to 60, an ATT and a short rationale.

The 13 settings map onto the method. Special predictors, predictors, their summary and their periods build X₁ and X₀. The controls identifier is the donor pool. custom_v either gives V or asks Synth to search it. The optimize periods set the pre‑treatment window whose outcome MSPE chooses V. Two settings pick the optimizers of the V search, and four set the quadratic program for the weights and its precision. The skill has no defaults. The fitter refuses a file with a missing or out‑of‑range field and names the field. Synth's own errors pass through as Synth prints them. On success, the fitter prints the predictors and V, the donor weights, the pre‑treatment fit, and for every period the observed outcome, the synthetic control and the gap. The harness records every fitter run, so the fit behind each answer can be identified afterwards. The skill also tells the model that pre‑treatment fit is an in‑sample quantity, and that a closer fit before treatment is not by itself a better estimate after it.

**8. scm‑design**

scm‑design hands the model the method's design in place of Synth's arguments. Its specification file holds four choices from page 496 of the paper. The predictors are either the two covariates with the outcome in every pre‑treatment period, or the covariates with a few weighted summaries of pre‑treatment outcomes. V is set by judgement with one weight per predictor, chosen so that the synthetic control fits the pre‑treatment outcome, or chosen on a validation stretch. Under validation the donor weights are fitted on a training period that ends at a chosen period, and V is chosen to fit the periods after it. The weights are convex, or they sum to one with any weight allowed below zero or above one, which the paper describes as allowing extrapolation. The penalty is none, or λ times each donor's weight times its squared distance from the treated unit's predictors, which keeps distant donors out and requires convex weights. Every fit uses all 29 donors and Synth's default optimizers and solver settings. The fitter refuses a specification that breaks a rule and names the field. At σ = 1 three of the four scm‑design answers took the outcome in every pre‑treatment period as predictors, with V fitted to the pre‑treatment outcome. GLM‑5.3 on DGP 2 took three outcome summaries, with V chosen on a validation stretch. All four chose convex weights and no penalty. The skill's own name, which the models read, is scm‑four‑design, and the deck calls it scm‑design.

**9. Profile skills**

The profile skills come from SkillFoundry, a framework from Carnegie Mellon that turns scientific resources such as papers, code and documentation into validated agent skills in a skill library. Its design‑skill stage runs a coding agent, here the Codex command line agent, with the generator model inside it. Both generators received the request on the slide, the paper's PDF and the source of Synth 1.1‑10, with R and Synth available. Each wrote a skill folder with its own SKILL.md and an R script into the SkillFoundry library, and the framework ran the library's validation checks on it. The generators named their skills themselves. GPT‑5.4 chose synth‑single‑treated‑unit‑scm, and DeepSeek chose synthetic‑control‑att‑starter.

A solver model under a profile skill reads the generated SKILL.md and calls the script with the panel, the treated periods and the output files. The script fits the synthetic control, computes the ATT from the counterfactual itself, and writes the answer file. In all 12 profile runs at σ = 1 the solver called the script at its default specification. So the three solver models gave one identical answer under each profile skill.

**10. Results, DGP 1, σ = 1**

The README column is a summary over 1,000 replications of the Section 3 estimator. Every other column is one answer on the shipped panel. Read an answer's ATT error against the README ATT RMSE, 0.317. That is the typical size of the Section 3 estimator's error at this noise level, even though its mean error is only −0.017. Only 60 percent of its replications land within 0.25 of −3.

All seven answers have positive errors, from +0.075 to +0.326. A positive error means the estimate understates the size of the reduction. Six of seven come within 0.25. The one outside is GLM‑5.3 under scm‑configure, at +0.326. GLM put weight on four donors there, so its random noise level is the highest, 1.170. All seven come within 0.50.

Both scm‑design answers equal the GPT‑5.4 profile skill's answer in every row. gpt‑oss‑120b and GLM‑5.3 both chose the covariates and the outcome in every pre‑treatment period, with V fitted to the pre‑treatment outcome, which on this panel gives the profile script's fit. These answers and GPT‑5.5 under scm‑configure have the lowest counterfactual RMSE, 0.992, and the lowest pre‑treatment RMSPE, 0.945. Their realized counterfactual RMSE lies below their own noise level, 1.095. A 20‑period realized RMSE can fall below its population noise level, and the README reports that this happens in 15 to 30 percent of replications at σ = 1. The DeepSeek profile skill uses only the two covariate means, its covariate balance is 0.000, and it spreads weight over all 29 donors. Its pre‑treatment RMSPE, 1.388, is among the highest, yet its ATT error is the smallest, +0.075. Each column is one draw of one procedure, so these gaps between answers do not rank the formats.

**11. Results, DGP 2, σ = 1**

The README estimator's RMSE is 0.507 here, and only 38 percent of its replications land within 0.25. One of seven answers comes within 0.25, GLM‑5.3 under scm‑configure at −0.045, which also has the lowest counterfactual RMSE, 0.771. GLM‑5.3 under scm‑design misses by 0.336. GPT‑5.5 under scm‑configure, gpt‑oss‑120b under scm‑design and the GPT‑5.4 profile skill share the same error, −0.516. They have the lowest pre‑treatment RMSPE, 0.734. On this panel the closest pre‑treatment fit did not give the closest counterfactual. Dropping one donor moves the DeepSeek profile skill's ATT by 0.560, the largest shift of any answer.

**17. How each reported ATT was written**

This slide covers all 54 answers at the three noise levels and looks at how the ATT in each answer file was produced. Each answer file is written by a shell command. A number that the model types into that command comes from its text generation. A number that the command computes comes from code. gpt‑oss‑120b typed its ATT as a literal number in 11 of its 12 configure and design answers, and 8 of those 11 differ from the mean gap of the answer's own counterfactual by more than 0.0001. In three of those eight, the typed number is the gap of a single period, 41 or 60, copied from the fitter's printed table. The other five appear in no earlier output of their run. The ATT was computed in code in 15 answers, by GLM‑5.3 under both skills, GPT‑5.5 under scm‑configure and gpt‑oss‑120b in one scm‑design answer, and two of them differ. Both are under scm‑design. gpt‑oss‑120b at σ = 0.5 on DGP 1 printed its counterfactual from an array that a separate awk call had filled, so every value came out 0, and its ATT came out 0. The ATT of that counterfactual is −2.977. GLM‑5.3 at σ = 0.2 on DGP 2 subtracted its counterfactual from the period 41 outcome in every period and reported −3.324, where its own counterfactual gives −3.102. A case ends at the step that writes a valid answer, so neither model saw the ATT it wrote. The profile scripts compute the ATT from the counterfactual in all 28 of their answers, and none differs. Every score uses the ATT computed from the counterfactual path.

**18. Findings**

These findings describe the σ = 1 panels. Under a profile skill the three models give one identical answer, because the script fixes the specification. Under scm‑configure the models choose different predictors, and the DGP 1 ATT error runs from 0.164 to 0.326 across models. Under scm‑design both models give the GPT‑5.4 profile skill's answer on DGP 1. Three of the four gpt‑oss‑120b answers under scm‑configure and scm‑design state an ATT that differs from the mean gap of its own counterfactual, by 0.38 to 1.58, and every other answer agrees with its counterfactual within 0.0001. On DGP 1, GPT‑5.5 under scm‑configure, both scm‑design answers and the GPT‑5.4 profile skill use 42 predictors, the two covariates and the outcome in each of periods 1 to 40, and reach the lowest counterfactual RMSE, 0.992. So the format decides who chooses the specification, and the profile format removes both the variation across models and the reporting errors.

**19. What each model chose**

This slide treats the specification as a result of the runs. Each row is one predictor set, with the number of predictors, how V was set, and who chose it. The collaborator chose the README Section 3 set, with V fixed at the identity. In scm‑configure and scm‑design the solver model picks the predictors at run time, and in a profile skill the generator picked them when it wrote the script.

Of the ten specifications the models chose at σ = 1, five give the GPT‑5.4 script's predictors, the covariate means and the outcome in each of periods 1 to 40. These are GPT‑5.5 under scm‑configure on both panels, gpt‑oss‑120b under scm‑design on both panels and GLM‑5.3 under scm‑design on DGP 1. gpt‑oss‑120b under scm‑configure used the two covariate means alone on DGP 2, the set the DeepSeek script fixes, and added the mean outcome on DGP 1. GLM‑5.3 under scm‑configure used four ten‑period outcome means on both panels. GLM‑5.3 under scm‑design on DGP 2 used three ten‑period outcome sums with the paper's validation choice of V, fitting the donor weights on periods 1 to 30 and V on periods 31 to 40.

Every answer let Synth search V and kept all 29 donors with convex weights and no penalty. Synth searches V in two nested steps. For a candidate V it fits the convex donor weights, then scores that synthetic control by the mean squared error of the treated unit's outcome over the chosen pre‑treatment periods, and an optimizer changes V until that error is smallest.
