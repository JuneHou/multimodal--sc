                                              Abstract

           We propose a synthetic historical control method for policy evaluation without
       relying on cross-sectional controls. Our approach builds on a semi-parametric time-
       series regression and follows the spirit of the conventional synthetic control method
       for identification, while replacing the treated unit with a treated block and the cross-
       sectional untreated units with a set of historical blocks from the same time series. We
       demonstrate that, under suitable conditions, the proposed method is approximately
       unbiased for estimating the intervention effect. Additionally, we show its effectiveness
       through simulation and real-world empirical applications. This method offers a
       valuable alternative for policy evaluation in scenarios where cross-sectional controls
       are unavailable.

Keywords: Historical control, Policy evaluation, Synthetic control.

1         Introduction

Panel-data methods for policy evaluation, or intervention analysis, require cross-sectional

untreated units to construct a control unit (or group). For instance, the difference-in-

differences (DID) design is widely used when there are substantial treated and untreated

units. This method relies on the “parallel-trend assumption,” which fails when the units

exhibit non-parallel trends in the absence of intervention. The synthetic control (SC)

method, introduced by Abadie & Gardeazabal (2003) and formalized by Abadie et al. (2010,

2015), works with one (or a few) treated unit(s) and relaxes the assumption to some extent

by predicting the counterfactual of treated unit using a convex combination of cross-sectional

untreated units; see Abadie (2021) for a survey and Hsiao et al. (2012), Xu (2017), Carvalho

et al. (2018) and Hsiao & Zhou (2019), among many others, for related methods.1

Although these methods are widely used to address empirical challenges in economics

and other social sciences, their use requires valid cross-sectional untreated units. This

prerequisite falls short when all units are treated. Acknowledging this problem is practical

and crucial. A notable example is an assessment of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic

on a country’s economic growth. As a global public health crisis, the pandemic potentially

affects all countries, making it challenging to define a set of cross-sectional untreated units

that remain unaffected by the pandemic. Another example is evaluating the effect of a

national policy change, such as a country-level minimum wage hike, a nationwide pension

for alternative approaches when all units are treated.

When cross-sectional untreated units are unavailable, it becomes essential to employ ap-

propriate time-series methods for policy evaluation. A common method is to directly

compare post-intervention outcomes with pre-intervention outcomes for the treated unit.

However, this (simple) pre/post comparison overlooks the fact that the temporal difference

in outcomes may reflect not only the intervention effect but also time-varying confounding.

The interrupted time series (ITS) design seeks to address this issue using a time-series

regression that includes a trend specification and its interaction with the intervention.

This design is considered a useful quasi-experimental method in the absence of cross-sectional

untreated units (Bloom 2003, Penfold & Zhang 2013, Kontopantelis et al. 2015), and is widely

applied across disciplines like epidemiology, public health, and economics.2 It is common to

specify a linear trend in this design. This specification reduces to the pre/post comparison

when the linear trend degenerates to a constant. The linear trend can be generalized by

incorporating a dynamic component, as in Box & Tiao (1975), or by replacing it with more

flexible basis-function specifications. For instance, a concurrent working paper Botosaru

et al. (2024) propose using polynomial or spline basis functions fitted via least squares on

the pre-intervention period, with the counterfactual predicted by extrapolating these fitted

basis functions.3 Alternatively, Brodersen et al. (2015) developed “CausalImpact,” which

In this paper, we propose a synthetic historical control (SHC) method for estimating the

time-varying intervention effect of a single treated unit, without relying on cross-sectional

untreated units. Our method is built on a semi-parametric time-series regression that incor-

porates a vector of control variables, a latent nonparametric time-varying component, and

a time-varying intervention effect. This semi-parametric design addresses the concern that

a parametric method may fail to identify the counterfactual when the trend is misspecified.

Importantly, the conventional semi-parametric method of Robinson (1988) is insufficient
the absence of intervention. However, unbiasedness requires the basis-function regression to be correctly

specified when the outcome sequence has a deterministic trend; see Botosaru et al. (2024, Assumption 4). See

also Cerqua et al. (2024) for a related application of machine learning methods in predicting counterfactuals

from pre-intervention time series.

for identification in this setting, as it does not separate the latent time-varying component

from the intervention effect in the post-intervention period. We address this limitation by

imposing smoothness on the latent component and identifying its post-intervention segment

in the spirit of the SC method, while replacing the treated unit with a “treated block” and

the cross-sectional untreated units with a set of “historical blocks” from the same time

series. Specifically, we partition the latent component sequence within the treated block

into a pre-intervention segment and the post-intervention segment. We then match the

pre-intervention segment using a convex combination of its historical counterparts, and

identify the post-intervention segment by applying the same combination to its historical

counterparts. This rationale underlies the name of the SHC method.

Compared to parametric methods, the SHC method does not pre-specify the latent compo-

nent, or the so-called trend, for extrapolation. Moreover, its matching quality fully relies

on the pre-intervention time series, and is therefore detectable through estimation. We

show that, under assumptions of smoothness and matching, the SHC method yields an

approximately unbiased estimator of the time-varying intervention effect, in the sense that

the bias diminishes as the post-intervention duration decreases or as the smoothness of the

latent component increases. We also provide a simple estimation method, and discuss the

choice of the treated and historical blocks.

We conduct Monte Carlo simulations to show that the SHC method considerably outperforms

several existing methods for identifying counterfactual using time-series data. We also

demonstrate the usefulness of the SHC method in real data using two case studies. In the

first case, we apply the SHC method to evaluate the impact of Brexit on the UK’s economic

growth. This is a case where valid cross-sectional untreated units are available for the SC

method; see Born et al. (2019). In this case, the SHC method provides estimates similar

to those obtained by the SC method even though only time-series data is used. In the



                                               4
second case, we apply the SHC method to assess the impact of COVID-19 on US economic

growth. This is a case lacking suitable cross-sectional untreated units for the SC method.

In this case, the SC method fails to deliver credible causal estimates, but the SHC method

offers reasonable results using only time-series data. The SHC method also generates more

sensible results than parametric time-series methods in both cases.

2.4     Choices of (m, n) and donor pool

The application of the SHC method requires specifying the post-intervention duration m and

the pre-intervention duration n to define the treated and historical blocks. In applications,

the choice of n is largely guided by the empirical context of policy evaluation and constrained

by the available time series data. For instance, a smaller (larger) n may be chosen when

the goal is to evaluate the intervention effect more promptly (retrospectively) after the

intervention, provided that sufficient data is available. Nonetheless, given (ϵ, H), the

asymptotic bias bound bϵ (H, k) showed in Proposition 2 increases with the post-intervention

horizon k ≤ n. From this perspective, the SHC method tends to favor a smaller n to ensure

lower potential bias in finite samples. Results based on a larger n should be interpreted

more cautiously, as they may be subject to greater bias.

Given a choice of n, we may select m based on two theoretical considerations. First, for

fixed (ϵ, k), the asymptotic bias bound bϵ (H, k) ultimately decreases with the degree of

smoothness H; see Figure 1. Moreover, Assumption 2(a) requires m ≥ H + 1. From this

perspective, a larger m is desirable. Second, the matching condition in Assumption 2(b)

is more likely to hold when ℓpre is low-dimensional. Since m is the dimension of ℓpre , a

smaller m is preferable from this viewpoint. To address the trade-off between these two

theoretical considerations, we measure the matching quality of ℓ̂pre (w) using the mean

4        Empirical Applications

This section demonstrates the effectiveness of the SHC method using the two case studies

mentioned in the Introduction: Brexit and COVID-19. For each case study, we illustrate

how the SHC method is implemented using real data and compare its empirical performance

with existing methods. Consistent with the simulation setup, we focus on a simplified case

that excludes xt . An extension that incorporates xt is provided in Section D of the Online

Appendix.



4.1        Brexit (Case with cross-sectional untreated units)

Brexit, the United Kingdom’s (UK) withdrawal from the European Union (EU), was decided

by an unexpected referendum result on June 23, 2016, leading to significant political and

economic consequences. Using the SC method, Born et al. (2019) provide among the first

use quarterly data from the OECD Economic Outlook database (November 2018 edition),

5      Conclusions

We propose the SHC method as an alternative to the panel data methods for policy evaluation

when the intervention affects all units. The SHC method is built on a semi-parametric time-

series regression that includes a latent component in addition to time-varying intervention

effects. By assuming that the latent component is a smooth function of time and that

its pre-intervention local trend can be matched by a convex combination of its historical

counterparts, the SHC method provides an approximately unbiased estimation of the

intervention effect. We also discuss the implementation issues and provide a stepwise-

matching method to construct a parsimonious donor pool for the SHC method. The

effectiveness of the SHC method is demonstrated through a Monte Carlo simulation and

two case studies: evaluating the impact of Brexit on UK economic growth and assessing

the impact of COVID-19 on US economic growth. In both case studies, the SHC method

performs well compared to existing methods, even when suitable cross-sectional untreated

units are unavailable in the case study on COVID-19. These findings highlight the usefulness

of the SHC method, especially when the intervention affects all units.

The SHC method is developed in the context of a single treated unit. In the presence of

multiple treated units, we may still apply the SHC method to each treated unit. Such an

application is useful for estimating potential heterogeneous intervention effects, regardless of

whether the units are treated at the same time. Additionally, the SHC method is established

for a single outcome variable; extending it to multiple outcomes could be both interesting

and useful. This might involve a multivariate semi-parametric time-series regression and a


                                              36
generalized matching condition. We leave this extension for future research.

A      Literature and Methods

Brodersen et al. (2015) developed “CausalImpact” and Taylor & Letham (2018) proposed

“Prophet” as time-series methods for intervention analysis and forecasting. These two meth-

ods are applied, respectively, at Google and Facebook, and offer more flexible specifications

than the standard ITS design.

More specifically, CausalImpact predicts the counterfactual using a Bayesian structural

time-series model with the state-space form:


                             yt = Zt⊤ αt + εt ,   αt+1 = Tt αt + Rt ηt ,                      (A.1)


where yt is the outcome variable, Zt is a d-dimensional observation vector that links the

observed data to the latent state vector, εt is a scalar observation error with noise variance

σt2 , αt is a d-dimensional latent state vector that comprises local linear trend, seasonal effects,

and contemporaneous covariates, Tt is a d × d state transition matrix determining how αt

evolves over time, Rt is a d × q control matrix that specifies how random disturbances enter

the state vector, and ηt is a q-dimensional system error vector which is assumed to follow

a multivariate normal distribution with zero mean and covariance matrix Qt . The model

integrates over uncertainty in parameters and state variables via Markov chain Monte Carlo


                                                  1
(MCMC) sampling to generate the posterior predictive distribution of the counterfactual.30

In comparison, Prophet predicts the counterfactual based on the time-series regression:


                                            yt = gt + st + ht + εt ,                                      (A.2)


where gt , st and ht are functions of time, with gt representing a logistic growth curve or a

piecewise linear function with automatic changepoint detection, st denoting the seasonality

component modeled via Fourier series, and ht characterizing the effects of holidays or events

via indicator variables, and εt is an error term. The model is implemented using either

maximum a posteriori or full Bayesian inference.31

In this study, we implement both methods using their respective R packages: CausalImpact

and prophet. The former provides functions for Bayesian structural time-series modeling

and causal inference, while the latter offers tools for decomposable time-series forecasting

with trend and seasonality components.

D.2     Extension: Including Control Variables

In this subsection, we extend our applications of the SHC method to include an additional

control variable xt . Specifically, we consider the case where xt includes only the lagged

outcome variable yt−1 to control for serial correlation. Table D1 shows the results for

the case study on Brexit. When including yt−1 , the estimated negative effect of Brexit is

smaller, suggesting that Brexit had reduced the UK GDP growth rate by 1.01 percentage

points by the end of 2018. Table D2 shows the SHC estimates for the COVID-19 impact

when including the lagged outcome variable yt−1 . We find that these estimates are very

similar to our main findings presented in the main text. Overall, the estimates indicate

that accounting for this additional control variable does not considerably alter our main

conclusions about the economic impacts of Brexit and COVID-19.
